## Abstract

This research work has been conducted for Oulu University of Applied Sciences
(OUAS) for a bachelor's thesis project. Commissioner is from inside OUAS,
called Verkkokaupan tutkimusryhmä. The topic of humor was chosen in order to
broaden the understanding and appreciation towards humor in marketing
communications. The views, mechanics, and politics of humor interest the
author, and thus the research project was born. Humor is a powerful tool in
everyday life and conceals great potential utility in marketing communications,
which this research project aims to answer.

The outlining research questions are how humor could be utilized to benefit a
company's marketing communications, and what affects the quality of humor.
The research is carried out using various examples and notes in order to
create a guidebook-like work for better understanding of humor in marketing
communications. To reinforce the findings, a small-scale survey was conducted
at Oulu University of Applied Sciences, covering two multicultural groups of
students and one Finnish group — 52 respondents in total. Due to the small
database no firm conclusions should be drawn, but the findings should allow
greater understanding of the characteristics of humor and work as preliminary
research on the topic.

The research work follows through from theory to practice with a case example
analysis. The case example is Finnish army equipment retailer **Varusteleka**,
analyzed using an applied four P's model. Varusteleka is known for its use of
wit and humor in its marketing communications.

**Keywords:** Humor, marketing, case, example, research, work

## 1. Introduction

This research is directed at companies interested in utilizing humor as a
marketing communications tool, ultimately encouraging and helping firms
identify the possibility of humor and understand its power in marketing
communications. Humor may play a strong role in a firm's branding and help
the company acquire more market share, creating a stronger brand identity.

Humor has a number of positive psychological effects on individuals that can
be applied to business — such as the stronger brand identity mentioned above.
Humor may be a risky investment and should be approached cautiously, but if
applied appropriately it can benefit a company greatly. Any firm in any market
can use humor in its marketing communications, but not many choose to.

The research follows the potential, limitations, and efficiency of humor, and
how humor could be applied accordingly — emphasizing how humor may be used
as a differentiation tactic and to increase virality (e.g. word-of-mouth) to gain
competitive advantage. Applied marketing mix and a global approach to humor
are also covered.

The case company is Finnish military equipment e-retailer **Varusteleka**. In
2010 Varusteleka achieved the leading position among military equipment
retailers in the EU. Varusteleka mainly operates in the Finnish market —
competing in a five-million-person market compared to much larger European
markets — and its emphasis is on product quality as well as clever use of humor
in marketing communications. Varusteleka operates a walk-in shop in Helsinki,
with most transactions made via their website; they accept international orders
but their main interest lies in the Finnish market.

Research questions:

1. What aspects have to be taken into account when using humor in marketing
   communications?
2. How can humor be utilized to benefit a company's marketing
   communications?

A survey was conducted among a multicultural group of students to gain
knowledge of how people from different backgrounds perceive humor. Case
example data was collected mainly from Varusteleka's webpages, correlated to
the research findings, and analyzed using the applied four P's paradigm.

## 2. Aspects of Humor

### 2.1 Definition of humor

Humor is highly diverse among people and even more diverse worldwide. There
are several unique types of humor that may be used appropriately in different
contexts, all aiming to provoke laughter or amusement (Gulas, Weinberger, &
March, 2006). Simply put: humor is the aspiration to provoke laughter and
amusement. "The response of laughter is based on two general situations.
Humor either plays a trick on the mind or it paints a picture which is ludicrous or
incongruous" (Audreith, 1998). Humor can take many forms: narrative jokes,
nonsensical slapstick, irony, and sarcasm (Bergeron & Vachon, 2008).

According to Taina Vuorela, humor is used in all cultures, is frequently
context-specific (making translation difficult), is based on cultural inside
knowledge, and reinforces feelings of togetherness within a group. If performed
appropriately, the customer base may feel solidarity between customers and the
company. A circle of friends has its own sense of humor depending on context
— the same applies to different cultures. International businesses may find it
very difficult to transfer humorous references into another language or culture
successfully; researching the target culture and language beforehand is
essential.

### 2.2 Effects of humor

Humor is known to be a powerful stress reliever, positively impacting a
person's physical and psychological well-being and feelings of hope (Vuorela,
2005; Bergeron & Vachon, 2008). The positive image created through humor
relates to trust generated by the good feeling it produces. Humor (feelings of
hope) decreases stress and perceived risk, potentially leading to purchase
decisions the customer would not normally make — this may decrease barriers
to purchase in e-retail. This advantage increases in cultures with a high
uncertainty-avoidance index (Hofstede, 2001).

Positive feelings via humor can create a psychological association between the
customer and the company. Barbara Plester (2009) suggests humor and fun may
be connected to improved performance and job satisfaction, and that use of
humor in the workplace may improve employee engagement. Bergeron and
Vachon further suggest that self-deprecating humor may be the most effective
tool for leaders and CEOs in gaining trust and credibility among workers.

### 2.3 Segmentation similarity

Humor's use is highly complex and contextual. "Whether or not something is
humorous depends on numerous factors, not the least of which is the nature of
the audience" (Gulas, Weinberger, & March, 2006). What's funny in one culture
may not be humorous in another; perception varies by gender, education level,
age group, and culture. A company must identify and understand its core
segment before using humor as a marketing tool — humor succeeds most when
the agent, the object of the humor, and the audience share commonality.

Example: Varusteleka knows its main customer base consists of males and may
choose highly masculine humor for bigger impact, voluntarily leaving out a
segment but strengthening the link with the dominant part of the market. This
isn't necessary — a softer delivery could appeal more broadly, at some cost to
initial impact.

Humor depends not just on the joke itself but on the interaction between joke,
joker, and audience — "a joke may be humorous from one source and highly
offensive from another" (Gulas, Weinberger, & March, 2006). Humor that
"victimizes" an opposing party (e.g. in gender roles) tends to correlate with a
more favorable response from the target audience, though negative disposition
should be used carefully since the affected secondary party will likely be
offended. For Varusteleka, shared interest in military equipment creates
commonality with existing/potential customers, letting humor land in the right
manner and proportion — i.e., deliver military-like jokes to military-like people.

### 2.4 Efficiency and opportunities

Humor's true potential relies on authenticity. Those who differentiate themselves
from the mass usually gain additional benefits. Applied correctly and in the right
proportion, humor can be an effective differentiation tactic and a strong
connection to customers. According to Bergeron and Vachon, humor increases
a person's credibility (linked to feelings of trust) and demonstrates humanity,
friendliness, and benevolence.

Humor may generate brand authenticity, making a company appear more
personal to its customer base, fostering closer connection, trust, and loyalty.
"Humor's ability to elicit positive emotions also has a positive correlation to
trust, which is a common issue for e-commerce websites" (Mallikarjunan, 2011).
Positive experiences shared by customers become free marketing via
word-of-mouth. Sylvie Jean (2011) suggests humor/humoristic brand image can
also be leveraged against competing brands via "brand parody" — deliberately,
acceptably mocking a competitor (e.g. the famous Audi–BMW chess-themed
billboard exchange, where BMW's "Checkmate" response won the exchange:
"appreciation should be maximal when our friends humiliate our enemies, and
minimal when our enemies manage to get the upper hand over our friends" —
Gulas, Weinberger, & March, 2006).

Humor can also negate the negative effects of errors. E-commerce is bound to
have bugs; humor in error messages can diffuse frustration — e.g. YouTube's
"a team of highly trained monkeys has been dispatched to deal with this
situation" instead of a bare 404 error (Bergeron & Vachon, 2008).

Humor is relatively cheap to produce and highly cost-effective for promoting a
company — but it's a double-edged sword: "people may laugh at the jokes but
forget the product telling it" (Fugate, 1998). The amount of humor is hard to
measure, but properly maintained it can yield high returns.

### 2.5 Limitations and risks

"In the case of a failed attempt at humor, the advertisers will pay a price" (Gulas,
Weinberger, & March, 2006). Consumers can be sensitive; humor may divide an
audience by gender, age, culture, education, and ethnicity, and requires
understanding the target group — without that knowledge a company is likely
to fail regardless of humor use. Subtle satire generally requires some
intelligence from the audience. Highly masculine humor may pressure a female
audience to feel they must belong, oppose, or be left out; younger audiences
tend to respond more strongly to humorous ads, with slang playing an important
role. Misused humor can reduce credibility, decrease trust, distort key
information, and create barriers between parties. Humor is not suitable for
every context — e.g. taboo contexts like funeral services.

### 2.6 Culture and humor

"A group has a culture when it has enough shared history to have formed a set
of basic assumptions which guide behavior, perception, thoughts and feelings"
— Barbara Plester, 2009. Humor in a culture can clarify a group's social and
moral boundaries and improve a sense of belonging/identity. A study found
Finnish and American college students respond similarly to humorous
advertising, suggesting shared humor preferences — though jokes may not
translate easily between languages (Gulas, Weinberger, & March, 2006).
Several scholars conclude humor is universal, with incongruity as its central
mechanism (Alden, Hoyer, & Lee, 1993) — no culture lacks a sense of humor,
but humor should not be applied generally without knowing the target audience,
especially where consequences could be serious.

Global advertising increases audience size and thus the challenge of
segmentation — the bigger the audience, the more likely humor won't reach
everyone. A company should figure out the maximum capacity of a joke (how
many people will understand it and its impact) and implement accordingly.
Hofstede's (2001) six cultural dimensions (power distance, individualism vs.
collectivism, masculinity vs. femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long- vs.
short-term orientation, indulgence vs. restraint) can help understand a target
culture and build appropriate incongruity into a joke. Offensive humor tends to
have a bigger impact in low uncertainty-avoidance cultures than high ones
(Hatzithomas, Zotos, & Boutsouki, 2011).

## 3. Humor in Marketing

### 3.1 Differentiation

Brands are a company's most valuable asset; clever brand management can be
key. Humor helps consumers recall a brand more clearly and convey messages
more efficiently (Gao, 2011). Once a brand is successfully differentiated as
humorous, that value compounds over time — consumers bring the expectation
of "funny" into future exposure, seeing later ads as funnier than they might
otherwise. Positive brand attitude makes humor a more effective persuasion
tool and improves brand evaluation.

E-retailers like Varusteleka can be seen as "faceless" — humor lets them arouse
emotion and communicate in ways that would otherwise be hard to achieve
without face-to-face service, helping build a credible, recognizable image. Since
e-retail differentiation otherwise relies mostly on product diversity, quality, or
price (all potentially costly), humor offers a cheap way to stand out and create
emotional connection.

A strong, humor-driven brand identity increases word-of-mouth — free
advertising, and arguably the most effective kind, since it comes from
uninterested third parties.

### 3.2 Humor in the consumer purchasing process

Five buying roles: initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, consumer (Kotler, 2003).
Humor can influence total customer value (the perceived bundle of economic,
functional, and psychological benefits) at each stage:

- **Initiator** — something amusing on a website can spark word-of-mouth that
  starts the information-gathering process; the company itself can also be the
  initiator.
- **Influencer** — a well-established humorous brand image can work as a
  strong influencer in the decision.
- **Decider** — humor can create a more positive image for whoever makes the
  final call.
- **Buyer** — trickier to influence via humor externally since the decision may
  already be made; more relevant in face-to-face negotiation, where humor can
  ease tension, build a positive image of the seller, and generate loyalty.
- **Consumer (end user)** — humor embedded in the product/service itself
  (especially easier in services, via face-to-face contact) raises perceived worth.

"Most authors claim that someone who is perceived as having a good sense of
humour is viewed as being competent, knowledgeable, happy, and socially
confident" (Bergeron & Vachon, 2008) — humor can be a gateway to a credible
salesperson image.

### 3.3 Word-of-Mouth

Getting customers to talk about the company themselves is the most personal,
intimate, and trusted form of marketing communication — since the party
sharing has no self-interest in promoting the company (Bergeron & Vachon,
2008).

## 4. Case Analysis of Varusteleka

A study was conducted at OUAS with an international group of 18–24-year-old
business students (52 respondents: 23 male / 9 female international, 5 male /
15 female Finnish) shown one of Varusteleka's marketing videos — a
Terminator parody. Respondents rated it 1–5 on: importance of humor in
marketing, frequency of watching commercials, humoristic value of the video,
efficiency estimate, and likelihood of visiting the website. (Video referenced:
youtube.com/watch?v=Dubpdh4x1So)

### 4.1 Gender roles

Males were more prone to watch funny commercials online than females; other
values stayed fairly constant between genders. Female respondents' qualitative
answers were mostly negative — several explicitly said they didn't feel part of
the target group or didn't find the video humorous/entertaining; one respondent
called it irritating. Several respondents (both genders) misread the video as
being for sunglasses and/or raised copyright-infringement concerns about the
Terminator reference, especially among those with no prior knowledge of
Varusteleka. Knowledge of the company strongly affected interpretation:
respondents (of either gender) who already knew Varusteleka rated it more
favorably, especially women — suggesting brand familiarity raises the perceived
value of the humor itself.

### 4.2 Cultural differences

International students were more likely to watch and share funny commercials
online than Finnish students (93% vs 70% had shared a funny commercial with
friends). Both groups valued humor in marketing similarly, but the international
group rated the video higher overall while more often *misunderstanding what
was actually being advertised* — the video seems to have been built assuming
Finnish cultural/company context that international viewers lacked. Finnish
respondents, even without prior company knowledge, generally understood the
core message — likely because "Varusteleka" itself ("roughly: equipment
hammer") telegraphs the business. The video appears to have been targeted
squarely at an already-Finnish, already-aware audience — a deliberate,
narrower design choice, not a mass-market miss.

### 4.3 Varusteleka analysis (applied four P's, minus Price, plus People)

- **Product** — e-retail can't let customers physically handle goods, raising
  perceived risk; humor in product *descriptions* lowers anxiety/risk and keeps
  visitors on-page longer. Concrete example: Varusteleka marketed a fur hat as
  literally **"unethical,"** using wit/incongruity in the description — an ordinary,
  price-premium product that sold out within days purely off the strength of
  the copy. It's no longer in the catalog, but the listing/description stayed live
  for existing and potential customers to find and enjoy.
  (`varusteleka.com/en/product/turkishattu-janiksesta-epaeettinen/11125/`,
  checked 27.11.2013)
- **Place** — layout/color scheme/banners matter; research audience taste
  before applying humor here to avoid misinterpretation.
- **Promotion** — humor should run through the whole company, not just be
  bolted onto ads — marketing communications should be an *extension* of an
  already-humorous company culture, not the source of it. Multiple staff writing
  Varusteleka's product listings all carry humor in their own individual voice,
  suggesting Varusteleka deliberately hires/encourages people with a sense of
  humor rather than centrally scripting jokes.
- **People** — in a service situation, humor can act as a pleasant distraction
  that lowers a customer's perceptual defenses before a purchase decision
  (parallel: a salesperson's manner affecting perceived value of an intangible
  product like insurance).

### 4.4 Findings

The Terminator-parody video would likely have failed outside Finland — several
non-Finnish respondents misread the message entirely despite rating it more
positively, while Finnish respondents rated it lower but understood it correctly.
Several respondents (of any background) felt excluded from the target
audience or read the parody as copyright infringement rather than homage.
With adaptation for context, the underlying humor style could likely translate
internationally too — foreign respondents actually rated it higher on average,
just without getting the joke.

A directly proposed idea: **Varusteleka could benefit from a recurring brand
mascot/spokesperson** — the thesis's own suggested example is **"Bill, the
lazy soldier,"** appearing across ads and the website until simply *seeing Bill*
recalls the brand, functioning like a logo but carrying extra marketing value
(brand parody potential, meme potential, word-of-mouth).

## 5. Conclusion and Discussion

Aspects affecting humor's impact: delivery, target group and its size, cultural
differences, past experience, intelligence, the joker, situation, context, and
general appreciation of humor — all roughly equally important; neglecting any
weakens the outcome. Humor is highly context-specific and impossible to
properly gauge without knowing the audience.

Major benefits identified: stronger brand identity/recognition, word-of-mouth,
greater customer involvement/solidarity, improved credibility, lower perceived
consumer risk, improved workplace performance/satisfaction, differentiation and
innovation, leverage against competitors (brand parody), and increased total
customer value.

Overall: humor has strong potential in marketing communications with
relatively few hard restrictions, but *how* it's implemented and its quality
determine the outcome. "Generally it takes a sense of humor to know humor,
but humor also can be studied." Used consistently and legitimately, humor can
function as both a marketing tool and a genuine internal job-performance/morale
enhancer at the same time.

An observation from the research process itself: people reacted more warmly
and openly the moment they simply *heard the topic* of this thesis, even before
any humor was actually used in discussing it — suggesting the mere
"implication of humor" primes people to be more receptive, and that reputation
for humor becomes self-reinforcing over repeated encounters.

Limitations: small survey sample size limits how conclusive the findings are;
further research could use a larger sample or study correlations between
personality traits and receptiveness to humor.
